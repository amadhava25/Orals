\section{General Analytic Results}

In this subsection, we will be introducing some general analytic results in functional analysis and harmonic analysis, that we will later use in our study of the (non)linear wave equations (and hyperbolic PDE, in general) \cite{Stein}. 

\subsection{Distributions and Generalized Functions} 

In this subsection, we introduce the notion of \textit{distributions}, which will become essential in our future study of nonlinear wave equations. Classically, we are used to the idea of a function $f$ (on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$) as assigning a definite value $f(x)$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. But we also saw in measure theory that it suffices to define a function up to a set of measure zero (i.e., we can allow $f$ to remain undefined on points contained in a set of measure zero).\footnote{which is to say that a function is really just an equivalence class of functions that agree pointwise a.e.} Now, we will see that it suffices to relax this definition even more in certain cases, leading to the idea of \textbf{distributions} or \textbf{generalized functions}. Rather than assigning a definite value at each point in its domain, we define a distribution by its averages taken with respect to a certain class of test functions (e.g., $C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$). As we will see in Section \S.\ref{sec:lwe}, one might not have classical solutions of desired regularity (in the sense of ordinary functions) for various partial differential equations. However, there could exist generalized functions, that although do not necessarily have the regularity to satisfy the original differential equations, that satisfy other properties that a normal classical solution would have. For example, consider the one-dimensional wave equation, 
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0.  
\end{equation*}
Multiplying both sides by an arbitrary smooth function, $\varphi(x,t)$ with compact support, and integrating one has, 
\begin{equation*}
	\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\varphi(x, t)\;dtdx + \int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\varphi(x, t)\;dtdx. 
\end{equation*}
Using Fubini's theorem, and integration by parts, one obtains 
\begin{equation*}
	\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t, x) \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial t}\;dtdx + \int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t, x)\frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial x}\;dtdx = 0. 
\end{equation*}
Thus any solution to the wave equation satisfies the above equation as long as it is at least $C^{1}$. However, it is possible that there exist functions $u$ that satify the above equation for all such $\varphi$ but is possibly not differentiable (so that $u$ does not satisfy the wave equation, itself). Hence, as we will see, distributions enable us to make sense of such $u$ (which we shall call \textit{weak solutions} to a differential equation). 

Thus we have the following definitions. 

\begin{definition}[Test Functions on $\Omega$]
Fix an open set $\Omega$ in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$. The \textbf{test functions} are those functions $\varphi \in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)$ (or equivalently, $C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$) - the set of all smooth functions with compact support in $\Omega$. We denote the space of all test functions on $\Omega$ by the symbol $\mathcal{D}(\Omega)$. 
\end{definition} 

\begin{definition}[Convergence in $\mathcal{D}(\Omega)$]
	Let $\{\varphi_{n}\} \subset \mathcal{D}(\Omega)$ and $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}$. $\varphi_{n}$ is said to converge to $\varphi$ if the supports of $\varphi_{n}$ are contained in a common compact set, and for every multiindex $\alpha$, one has that $\partial_{x}^{\alpha} \varphi_{n} \to \partial_{x}^{\alpha}\varphi$ uniformly in $x$ as $n \to \infty$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Distributions on $\Omega$]
	A \textbf{distribution} $F$ on $\Omega$ is a complex-valued linear functional $\varphi \mapsto F(\varphi)$, defined for all $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}$, that is continuous in the sense that $F(\varphi_{n}) \to F(\varphi)$ whenever $\varphi_{n}\to \varphi$ in $\mathcal{D}$. We shall denote the vector space of all distributions on $\Omega$ by $\mathcal{D}^{\ast}(\Omega)$. 
\end{definition} 

For example, all signed Radon measures on an open subset $\Omega$, and all locally integrable function on $\Omega$ are examples of distributions on $\Omega$. Our ultimate goal is to ``calculus'' using distributions. For smooth functions $f$, one of the most important results we have (as we shall see in the Section \S.\ref{sec:lwe}) is \textbf{integration by parts}: for any $f \in C^{\infty}(\Omega)$ and $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(\Omega)$, a simple inductive argument yields, 
\begin{equation*}
	\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f)\varphi(x)\;dx = (-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_{\Omega}f(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\varphi)\;dx,  
\end{equation*}
where the boundary terms are identically zero because $\varphi$ has compact support. Thus, generalizing this definition, 

\begin{definition}[Derivative of a Distribution]
	If $F \in \mathcal{D}^{\ast}(\Omega)$, for any multiindex $\alpha$, one defines the \textbf{distributional derivative of $F$}, $(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}F)$ as follows: for any $\varphi \in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)$, 
	\begin{equation}
		(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}F)(\varphi) = (-1)^{|\alpha|}F(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\varphi). 
		\label{eq:dist_der}
	\end{equation}
\end{definition}
Thus while an arbitrary locally integrable function $f$ may not have sufficient regularity to talk about its classical derivatives, we can certainly talk about its partial derivatives as distributions (which exist). 

We can generalize some basic properties of functions to distributions. 

\begin{definition}[Some Basic Operations on Distributions]
	Let $\Omega$ be an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, and let $F \in \mathcal{D}^{\ast}$. Then we define the following. \vspace{-1.2em}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt]
		\item If $\psi \in C^{\infty}(\Omega)$, then the product $\psi \cdot F$ is defined by $(\psi \cdot F)(\varphi) \coloneqq F(\psi \varphi)$ for every $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}$. It stands that $\psi \cdot F \in \mathcal{D}^{\ast}$. 
		\item Let $\tau_{\empspace}$ denote the translation operator: $\tau_{h}f(x) \coloneqq f(x - h)$ for any $h \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$. Then the corresponding definition for distributions is, 
		\begin{equation*}
			\tau_{h}(F)(\varphi) = F(\tau_{-h}\varphi), \qquad \forall \varphi \in \mathcal{D}. 
		\end{equation*}
		\item If $L$ is a \textit{nonsingular} linear transformation on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, then if $f_{L}(x) \coloneqq f(Lx)$ for functions, on distributions, one has 
			\begin{equation*}
				F_{L}(\varphi) = |\det{L}|^{-1} \cdot F(\varphi_{L^{-1}}), \qquad \forall \varphi \in \mathcal{D}. 
			\end{equation*}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Another property we would like to generalize is convolution of a distribution with a test function. We make the following definitions: 

\begin{definition}[Convolution of a Distribution with Test Functions]
	Let $F \in \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$, and $\psi \in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$. Then we make the definitions, \vspace{-1.2em}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt]
		\item (As a function): $(F \ast \psi)(x) \coloneqq F(\psi^{\ti{}}_{x})$, where $\psi_{x}^{\ti{}} \coloneqq \psi(x - y)$, 
		\item (As a distribution): $(F \ast \psi)(\varphi) \coloneqq F(\psi^{\ti{}} \ast \varphi)$, for all $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$, with $\psi^{\ti{}} \equiv \psi^{\ti{}}_{0}$. 
	\end{enumerate}
\end{definition} 

To see where these definitions could come from, assume first that $F$ is a locally integrable function. Then one has that 
\begin{equation*}
	(F \ast \psi)(x) \coloneqq \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x - y)\psi(y)\;dy = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(y)\psi(x - y)\;dy. 
\end{equation*}
This leads naturally to the first definition. On the other hand, for any $\varphi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$, if $F$ is a locally integrable function, then 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		(F \ast \psi)(\varphi) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(y)\psi(x - y)\varphi(x)\;dydx \\
		&= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(y)\psi(x - y)\varphi(x)\;dxdy \qquad (\text{by Fubini's Theorem}) \\
		&= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(y)(\psi^{\ti{}}\ast \varphi)(y)\;dy  \\
		&= F(\psi^{\ti{}} \ast \varphi). 
	\end{split} 
\end{align*}
This leads naturally to the second definition. 

\begin{prop}[Equivalence of Definitions of Convolution]
	Suppose $F$ is a distribution and $\psi \in \mathcal{D}$. Then \vspace{-1.75em}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt]
		\item The two definitions of $F \ast \psi$ given above coincide. 
		\item The distribution $F \ast \psi$ is a $C^{\infty}$ function. 
	\end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\section{Linear Wave Equation}\label{sec:lwe}

\subsection{Introduction} 
Through these notes, our goal is to study the properties of the nonlinear wave equation, which can either be a ``simple'' scalar equation or a system of equations \cite{Luk}: 
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det(g)}}\partial_{\mu}\left(\sqrt{-\det{g}}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi\right) = F(\phi, \partial \phi) 
	\label{eq:nlw}, 
\end{equation}
where $g$ is a Lorentzian metric on $I \times \mathbb{R}^{n}$, and $\phi: I \times \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}$ is an unknown function of some regularity. For example, if $n = 1$, and $g$ is the flat Minkowski metric, $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, then the nonlinear wave equation is merely 
\begin{equation*}
	\partial_{\mu}(\partial^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi) = F(\phi, \partial \phi) \iff \partial_{t}^{2} - (\partial_{x}\phi)^{2} = F(\phi, \partial \phi). 
\end{equation*}
Note that we denote the coordinate of $I$ by $t$ (to designate the \textit{time} coordinate), and the coordinates of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ by $x = (x^{1}, \ldots, x^{n})$. An equation of the form (\ref{eq:nlw}) is said to be \vspace{-1.2em}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\roman{*})]
	\item \textbf{linear}: $g$ is independent of $\phi$ and $F$ is a linear function of both of its arguments;
	\item \textbf{semilinear}: $g$ is independent of $\phi$  and $F$ is a nonlinear function; 
	\item \textbf{quasilinear}: $g$ is independent of $\phi$ and/or $\partial \phi$. 
\end{enumerate}
Before we build up to the nonlinear wave equation, we will first examine the properties of the linear wave equation. Generally speaking, we are interested in the following properties: \vspace{-1.2em}
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\roman{*})]
	\item \textbf{Existence and Uniqueness}: Does a solution to (\ref{eq:nlw}) exist, and if so, is it the only solution? 
	\item \textbf{Dispersion}: What physical properties do solutions to (\ref{eq:nlw}) have? 
	\item \textbf{Well-Posedness}: If we consider the \textit{Cauchy wave problem} consisting of the equation (\ref{eq:nlw}) along with initial data $(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)$ on the hypersurface $\{t = 0\}$, where $\phi, \partial_{t}\phi$, does there exist a unique solution to the problem that satisfy the initial conditions and is continuously dependent on the initial data?
\end{enumerate}

We will gradually work through each of these results, sometimes developing new techniques and machinery. We first begin by developing some general analytic results, and idea of energy estimates. Then we will apply these to the study of linear wave equations. This will then give a foundation for the study of nonlinear wave equations.

\subsection{Energy Estimates}

In this section, we introduce the notion of the energy of a solution to the wave equation. We begin by considering the homogeneous linear Cauchy problem, 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{1}
		&\Box \phi = 0, \\
		&(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}), 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
	\label{eq:clwp}
\end{equation}
where we initially assume $\phi_{0}, \phi_{1} \in C_{c}^{\infty} \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, and $\Box$ denotes the \textbf{wave operator}. As of now, we do not yet have an existence (or uniqueness) result for this Cauchy wave problem. Thus we simply assume that such a unique (sufficiently regular) solution exists for now. Then we define the \textbf{energy} of a solution to be the following $L^{2}$-type norm,
\begin{definition}[Energy]
	For $(\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}) \in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ and $\phi$ the solution to the Cauchy problem (\ref{eq:clwp}), the \textbf{energy} of $\phi$ at the spacetime point $(t, x)$ is defined to be the $L^{2}$-type norm, 
	\begin{equation}
		E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left((\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\right)(t, x)\;dx. 
		\label{eq:energy_lwp}
	\end{equation}
	\label{def:energy}
\end{definition}
Intuitively, the definition for the energy is as we expected: consider an oscillating 1-dimensional string, and let $\phi(t, x)$ denote the amplitude of the wave at a point $x$ at time $t$. Then the quantity $(1/2)(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}$ resembles the kinetic energy of an infinitesimal point particle at the spacetime location $(t, x)$, while the quantity $\sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}$ resembles the (spring) potential energy at $(t, x)$. Thus under these observations, $E(t)$ resembles the total energy of the system at some time $t$. Since the Cauchy wave problem (\ref{eq:clwp}) has no source term (i.e., is homogeneous), one expects the following result \cite{Luk}: 
\begin{prop}[Conservation of Energy]
	Let $\phi$ be a (sufficiently regular) solution to (\ref{eq:clwp}). Define $E(t)$ as given in Definition \ref{def:energy}. Then $E$ is constant in time. 
	\label{prop:cons_energy}
\end{prop} 

\begin{remark}
		We will give two proofs for this result; one relies on straightforward differentiation of $E(t)$ and follows the steps outlined in \cite{Luk}, while the other introduces the concept of conservation laws and follows the steps outlined in \cite{Lawrie}. 
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof \# 1]
	Let $\phi$ be a sufficiently regular solution to (2), and define the energy $E(t)$ of $\phi$ to be the function, 
	\begin{equation*}
		E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left((\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\right)(t, x)\;dx, 
	\end{equation*}
	where we assume that the initial data $\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}$ are both smooth with compact support. As we will see in a later section, $\phi \in C^{\infty}(I, \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))$. Thus by the Dominated Convergence Theorem allows us to pass a time derivative inside the integral sign. We then obtain, 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			\frac{d}{dt}E(t) &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{t}^{2}\phi) + \sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{t}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\;dx \\
			&= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)\cdot \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\right) + \sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{t}\phi \cdot (\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\;dx  \\
			&= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{j}(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)\;dx  \\
			&= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{1}^{n}\left[(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)\bigg|_{|x| = \infty} - \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}^{2} \phi)\;dx\right] \\
			&= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\right)\;dx - \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\right)\;dx = 0, 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	where the boundary term is identically zero since $\phi$ is Schwartz. Thus, the energy is conserved. 
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof \# 2]
	We start by claiming that solutions to (\ref{eq:clwp}) are the stationary points of a variational integral. Define the linear functional $\mathcal{L}: C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})) \to \mathbb{R}$ by 
	\begin{equation*}
		\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\;dtdx; 
	\end{equation*}
	to obtain this functional, we can use the Euler-Lagrange equations for a physical system. Now let $\psi \in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^{n})$. We recall that $\braket{\mathcal{L}'(\phi), \psi}$, where $\mathcal{L'}(\phi)$ is the Fr\'echet derivative is defined as the directional derivative along $\psi$; i.e., 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			\braket{\mathcal{L}'(\phi), \psi} &= \frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{L}(\phi + \epsilon \psi)\bigg|_{\epsilon = 0} \\
			&= \frac{d}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon = 0} \left(\mathcal{L}(\phi) + \epsilon \int_{\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n} (-\nabla \phi \cdot \nabla \psi + (\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{t}\psi))\;dtdx + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)\right) \\
			&= \int_{\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}-(\nabla \phi) \cdot (\nabla \psi) + (\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{t}\psi)\;dtdx. 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*} 
	Since $\psi$ is smooth with compact support, and $\phi$ is Schwartz, using Fubini's theorem and integration by parts, and recognizing that the boundary terms are identically zero, one obtains 
	\begin{equation*}
		\braket{\mathcal{L}'(\phi), \psi} = -\int_{\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}(\Box \phi) \psi\;dtdx = 0. 
	\end{equation*}
	Thus since $\phi \in C_{c}^{\infty}$ was arbitrary, we conclude that $\phi$ is stationary for $\mathcal{L}$ iff $\Box \phi = 0$. Now, we recall N\"other's theorem, which states that if $\mathcal{L}$ is invariant with respect to a 1-parameter family of diffeomorphisms, then there exists a conservation law for an extreme value of $\mathcal{L}$. We note that $\mathcal{L}$ is time-translation invariant: if $G_{\epsilon}: \phi(t, x) \mapsto \phi(t + \epsilon, x)$, then one observes that $\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathcal{L}(G_{\epsilon}\phi)$. Therefore, since the vector field $\partial_{t}$ generates the flow corresponding to time translations, one has 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			0 &= \phi_{t}\Box \phi \\
			&= \phi_{tt}\phi_{t}- \Delta \phi \phi_{t} \\
			&= \partial_{t}\left(\frac{(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}}{2} - \frac{(\nabla \phi)^{2}}{2}\right) - \operatorname{div}(\nabla \phi \phi_{t}). 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	Then, integrating over $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ yields, 
	\begin{equation*}
		0 = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - (\nabla \phi)^{2}\;dx\right) - \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\operatorname{div}(\nabla \phi \phi_{t})\;dx. 
	\end{equation*}
	The last term vanishes by using integration by parts and recalling that $\phi$ is Schwartz. Thus, one has that the energy is independent of time. 
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
	As the second proof shows, because of the time translation invariance, to show that the energy is conserved, it suffices to integrate the quantity $(\partial_{t}\phi)(\Box\phi)$ by parts. 
\end{remark}

Likewise, we have the following conservation law in Sobolev norms \cite{Holzegel2021}. 

\begin{corollary}[Sobolev Conservation Law I]
	Given initial data $(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, and assuming the existence of a solution $\phi \in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}; \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))$ to the Cauchy wave problem (\ref{eq:clwp}), for all $s \in \mathbb{R}$, 
	\begin{equation}
		\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{H^{s - 1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})} + \norm{\nabla \phi}_{H^{s - 1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})} = \norm{\phi_{0}}_{H^{s - 1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})} + \norm{\nabla \phi_{1}}_{H^{s - 1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}. 
	\end{equation}   
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
	Assume that $\phi \in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}; \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))$ is a solution to (\ref{eq:clwp}) with initial data $(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$. If $\hat{\empspace}$ denotes the Fourier transform of $\empspace$, then one has the equation
	\begin{equation*}
		(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + 4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}(\hat{\phi})^{2} = (\hat{\phi}_{0})^{2} + 4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}(\hat{\phi}_{1})^{2}.
	\end{equation*} 
	Note, this equation becomes clearer once we establish a representation formula for $\phi$ in a later section. Multiplying through by $(1 + |\xi|^{2})^{s - 1}$ (for arbitrary $s \in \mathbb{R}$), and integrating, yields the desired conservation law. 
\end{proof}

One immediate advantage of having conservation laws is that if the Cauchy problem (\ref{eq:clwp}) has a solution, then these laws immediately imply uniqueness.

To extract more useful information, we can localize the energy identity, thus obtaining the following result. 

\begin{prop}[Localized Energy Identity]
	Define, for $0 \leq t \leq R$, 
	\begin{equation}
		E(t;x, R) \coloneqq \frac{1}{2}\int_{B(x, R - t)}\left((\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\right)(t, y)\;dy. 
		\label{eq:local_e_id}
	\end{equation}
	Then $E(t;x, R)$ is a nonincreasing function of $t$. 
\end{prop} 

\begin{remark}
	This result tells us that the energy at time $t$ is less than or equal to the energy at time $s > t$ in the backwards light cone with vertex at $x$. 
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
	As suggested in the second proof of Proposition \ref{prop:cons_energy}, we will start with the expression $(\partial_{t}\phi)\Box\phi = 0$ and integrate by parts in the region to the future of $\{t_{1}\} \times B(x, R - t_{1})$ and to the past of $(\{t_{2}\} \times B(x, R - t_{2})) \cup \left(\bigcup_{s \in [t_{1}, t_{2}]}(\{s\} \times \partial B(x, R - s))\right)$. The idea behind this choice is that the boundary terms conveniently give us $E(t_{1}; x, R)$ and $E(t_{2}; x, R)$. The boundary term on $\bigcup_{s \in [t_{1}, t_{2}]}(\{s\} \times \partial B(x, R - s))$ then has a definite sign, which then allows us to compare the final and initial energy. Indeed, one has the following computations, 
	\begin{multline*}
			0 = \int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{B(x, R - t)}\left((\partial_{t}\phi)\left(-\partial_{t}^{2}\phi + \sum_{1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}\phi\right)\right)(t,y)\;dydt 
			\\ = -\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}} \int_{B(x, R - t)}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\right)\;dydt
			 + \sum_{1}^{n}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{B(x, R- t)}\partial_{j}((\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot (\partial_{j}\phi))\;dydt \\
			= -\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{B(x, R - t)}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}+ \Delta \phi \right]\;dt
			 - \frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial B(x, R - t)}\left((\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\right)
			\\ + \int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial B(x, R - t)}(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi) \cdot \frac{(y- x)_{j}}{|y - x|}\;d\sigma(y)dt \\
			= -E(t_{2}; x, R) + E(t_{1}; x, R) 
			- \frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial B(x, R - t)}\left((\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2} - 2(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \sum_{1}^{n}\frac{(y -x)_{j}}{|y - x|} (\partial_{j}\phi)\right)\;d\sigma(y)dt. 
	\end{multline*}
	where the second equality follows from Theorem C.4.6 of \cite{evans10}. Now we estimate the final term of the last equation. Cauchy-Schwarz tells us the following (CS arises in the second line), 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			2(\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \sum_{1}^{n}\frac{(y - x)_{j}}{|y - x|}(\partial_{j}\phi)&\leq (\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\frac{(y - x)_{j}}{|y - x|}(\partial_{j}\phi)\right)^{2}\\
			&\leq (\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \left(\sum_{1}^{n}\frac{(y - x)_{j}}{|y -x|}\right)^{2} \cdot \left(\sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}\right) \\
			&= (\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \sum_{1}^{n}(\partial_{j}\phi)^{2}. 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	Thus this implies that the final term is negative. So then, one must have $-E(t_{2}; x, R) + E(t_{1}; x, R) \geq 0$, whence $E(t_{2}; x, R) \leq E(t_{1}; x, R)$. 
	\end{proof}
	
While we are at it, we also consider the following linear, non-constant coefficient wave equation, 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{1}
		&\partial_{\alpha}(a^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\phi) = F, 	\\
		&(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}), 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
	\label{eq:clwp_ncc} 
\end{equation}
where $a$ is assumed to be a symmetric $(n + 1)\times (n + 1)$ matrix on $I \times \mathbb{R}^{n}$ satisfying 
\begin{equation*}
	\sum_{\alpha,\beta}|a^{\alpha\beta} - m^{\alpha\beta}| < \frac{1}{10}. 
\end{equation*}
While the energy is not conserved, in general, due to the source term $F$, the growth of the energy is controlled in the following sense. 

\begin{theorem}[Growth Bound on Energy]
	Assume $\phi$ is a solution to (\ref{eq:clwp_ncc}) of some sufficient regularity; let $E(t)$ be the energy of $\phi$ in the sense of Proposition (\ref{prop:cons_energy}). Then there exists a positive constant $C = C(n) > 0$ such that the following energy estimate holds 
	\begin{equation}
		\norm{E^{1/2}}_{C([0,T])} =  \leq C\left(\norm{(\phi_{0}, \phi_{1})}_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})} + \int_{0}^{T}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt\right)\exp\left(C\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\partial a}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt\right), 
		\label{eq:clwp_nc_ebound}
	\end{equation} 
	\label{thm:clwp_nc_ebound}
\end{theorem}
where by $\partial \empspace$, we denote $(\partial_{t} \empspace)^{2} + \Delta \empspace$. 

To prove this result, we first require \textbf{Gr\"onwall's lemma}. 

\begin{lemma}[Gr\"onwall's Lemma]
	Let $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a nonnegative continuous function and $g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a nonnegative integrable function such that 
	\begin{equation*}
		f(t) \leq A + \int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(s)\;ds
	\end{equation*}
	holds for some $A \geq 0$, and every $t \in [0,T]$. Then 
	\begin{equation*}
		f(t) \leq A \exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\;ds\right)
	\end{equation*}
	for every $t \in [0,T]$. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	$ $\newline \vspace{-0.65cm}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\arabic{*})]
		\item (Standard Proof): The proof follows by showing that the result holds for any $A > 0$, and then passing to the limit $A \to 0$. First, we compute 
		\begin{equation*}
			\frac{d}{dt}\left(A + \int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(s)\;ds\right) = f(t)g(t) \leq g(t)\left(A + \int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(s)\;ds\right). 
		\end{equation*}
		In particular, this implies that 
		\begin{equation*}
			\frac{d}{dr}\log\left[A + \int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(s)\;ds\right] \leq g(r), 
		\end{equation*}
		so that integrating, 
		\begin{equation*}
			\log\left[A + \int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(s)\;ds\right] \leq \int_{0}^{t}g(r)\;dr + \log{A}. 
		\end{equation*}
		Thus, we conclude that 
		\begin{equation*}
			f(t) \leq A + \int_{0}^{t}f(s)g(s)\;ds \leq A\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}g(r)\;dr\right). 
		\end{equation*}
		\item (Bootstrap Method): Now we will prove the same result again, using the \textbf{bootstrap} method. For each $\epsilon > 0$, consider the following condition: 
		\begin{equation*}
			f(t) \leq (1 + \epsilon)A\exp\left((1 + \epsilon)\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\;ds\right), 
			\tag{$\star$}
		\end{equation*}
		and define the subset $B \subseteq [0,T]$ by 
		\begin{equation*}
			B \coloneqq \{t \in [0,T]: (\star) \text{ holds for all $s \in [0,t]$}\}. 
		\end{equation*}
		It follows immediately that $B$ is nonempty as it at least contains the point $0 \in B$. Likewise, by continuity of $f$, it follows that $B$ is closed. By continuity of $f$, if we can show that $f$ satisfies a \textit{better} estimate for $t \in B$, then it must follow that $f$ satisfies the above bound in some small neighborhood of $t$, which would show that $B$ is open in $[0,T]$. Indeed, let $t \in B$. Then combining the hypothesis, one has that 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				f(t) &\leq A + \int_{0}^{t}g(s)\;ds \leq A + (1 + \epsilon)A\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\exp\left((1 + \epsilon)\int_{0}^{s}g(r)\;dr\right)\;ds \\
				&\leq A\left(1 + \left(\exp\left((1 + \epsilon)\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\;ds\right)-1\right)\right) \qquad (\text{by integration by parts}) \\
				&= A\exp\left((1 + \epsilon)\int_{0}^{t}g(s)\;ds\right). 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		Thus, this shows that $B$ must be open. Since $[0,T]$ is connected, one must have $B = [0,T]$. Hence the modified condition holds for all $t \in [0,T]$ and $\epsilon > 0$ so that passing to the limit $\epsilon \to 0$ yields the desired estimate. 
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

Now we will use Gr\"onwall's lemma to prove the energy estimate from above. 

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:clwp_nc_ebound}]
	For the sake of this proof, we will be assuming that $\phi$ is compactly supported so that we can ignore the spatial boundary terms in the integration. The fundamental idea behind the techniques in this proof is to bound the energy of the solution on the time interval $[0,T]$ by looking at the total energy at some fixed time, and then summing these contributions. In other words, we wish to integrate the first term of the quantity, $\partial_{t}\phi(\partial_{\alpha}(a^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\phi) - F)$ by parts. Before doing so, we make the following observations. 
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\arabic{*})]
		\item Suppose first that $\alpha = \beta = 0$. Then one has that 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				\partial_{t}\phi(\partial_{\alpha}(a^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\phi)) &= (\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{t}(a^{tt}\partial_{t}\phi)) \\
				&= (\partial_{t}\phi)((\partial_{t}a^{tt})(\partial_{t}\phi)) + a^{tt}(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{tt}\phi) \\
				&= (\partial_{t}a^{tt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \frac{1}{2}a^{tt}\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}, 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		so that 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{t}(a^{tt}\partial_{t}\phi))\;dxdt &= \int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}} (\partial_{t}a^{tt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + \frac{1}{2}a^{tt}\partial_{t}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx dt \\
				&= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\{T\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{tt}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx - \frac{1}{2}\int_{\{0\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{tt}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx \\
				&\qquad + \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}a^{tt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dxdt, 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		where the second line follows from Fubini/Tonelli, and the fundamental theorem of calculus. 
		
		\item Now assume that one of the indices is $t$, while the other index runs from $j = 1, \ldots, n$. Then one has that 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				\partial_{t}\phi(\partial_{\alpha}(a^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\phi)) &= (\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \left[\partial_{j}(a^{jt}\partial_{t}\phi) + \partial_{t}(a^{tj}\partial_{j}\phi)\right] \\
				&= (\partial_{t}\phi) \cdot \left[(\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi) + a^{jt}\partial_{j}(\partial_{t}\phi) + \partial_{t}(a^{jt})(\partial_{j}\phi) + a^{jt}\partial_{j}(\partial_{t}\phi)\right] \\
				&= (\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + a^{jt}\partial_{j}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + (\partial_{t}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi). 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		Observe that $a^{jt} = a^{tj}$ by symmetry of $a$. We first consider the integral of the middle term. Integration by parts tells us that 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{jt}\partial_{j}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx dt &= \int_{0}^{T} \left[a^{jt}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}|_{\text{bdr.}} - \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx\right]\;dt \\
				&= -\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dxdt, 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		where the boundary terms vanish because of our assumption that $\phi$ is compactly supported. Thus in fact, 
		\begin{equation*}
			\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}\phi(\partial_{\alpha}(a^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\phi))\;dxdt = \int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - (\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} + (\partial_{t}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)\;dx dt. 
		\end{equation*}
		\item Now assume that both indices run over $i, j = 1, \ldots, n$. Then one has that  
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}\phi (\partial_{i}(a^{ij}\partial_{j}\phi))\;dxdt &= (\partial_{t}\phi)(a^{ij}\partial_{j}\phi)\bigg|_{\text{bdr.}} - \int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}\partial_{i}\phi a^{ij}\partial_{j}\phi\;dx dt \\
				&= -\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}\partial_{t}(\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi)\;dxdt  \\
				&= -\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{T\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}\partial_{i}\phi \partial_{j}\phi\;dx + \frac{1}{2}\int_{\{0\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi \;dx  \\ &\qquad\quad + \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}(\partial_{t}a^{ij}(\partial_{i}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)\;dxdt, 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		where the final line follows from the fact that 
		\begin{equation*}
			\partial_{t}(a^{ij}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi) - (\partial_{t}a^{ij})(\partial_{i}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi) = a^{ij}\partial_{t}(\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi), 
		\end{equation*}
		we used the fundamental theorem of calculus after using the above fact, and the boundary terms in the first line vanish since $\phi$ has compact support. 
	\end{enumerate}
	Now we obtain the following result: 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{\alpha}(a^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\phi)) &= \int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)F \\
			\implies 
			\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{T\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{tt}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx -\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{T\}\times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\;dx &= \frac{1}{2}\int_{\{0\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{tt}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx  -\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{0\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\;dx \\
			&\hspace{5pt}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)F - \frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}a^{tt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - (\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} \\
			&\qquad\qquad\hspace{2pt}+(\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - (\partial_{t}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)\;dxdt
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	Now, one has that 
	\begin{multline*}
		\abs{\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\partial_{t}\phi)F - \frac{1}{2}(\partial_{t}a^{tt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - (\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}+(\partial_{j}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2} - (\partial_{t}a^{jt})(\partial_{t}\phi)(\partial_{j}\phi)\;dxdt} \\
		\leq 
		C'\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t) + \norm{\partial a}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\norm{\partial \phi}^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt, 
	\end{multline*}
	where we used H\"older's inequality to obtain the first term, and assumed $\partial a \in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$. 
	On the other hand, by our hypothesis on $a^{\alpha\beta}$, there exists some constant $K > 0$ large enough so that (taking $C = \min\{C',K\}$)\todo{Explain how?},
	\begin{equation*}
		\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(T) \leq C\abs{\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{T\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{tt}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}\;dx - \frac{1}{2}\int_{\{T\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}\partial_{i}\phi\partial_{j}\phi\;dx}. 
	\end{equation*}
	Thus this implies that 
	\begin{multline*}
		\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(T) \leq C\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}(0) + C\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t) + \norm{\partial a}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\norm{\partial \phi}^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt. 
	\end{multline*}
	Since one can repeat this argument for any smaller interval $[0, T']$, where $0 < T' < T$, one obtains 
	\begin{multline*}
		\sup_{t \in [0,T]}\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t) \leq C\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}(0) + C\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t) + \norm{\partial a}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\norm{\partial \phi}^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt. 
	\end{multline*}
	For the first term inside the integral, one has that 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt &\leq \sup_{t \in [0,T]}\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})} \cdot \left(\int_{0}^{T}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt\right)\\
			&\leq \delta\norm{\partial_{t}\phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2} + \frac{1}{4\delta}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt\right)^{2}. 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	where the second line follows from Young's inequality with $\epsilon$\footnote{For any $a, b \geq 0$ and any $\epsilon > 0$, $$ab \leq \frac{\epsilon^{p}a^{p}}{p} + \frac{1}{e^{q}q}b^{q}$$, where $p$, $q$ are conjugate exponents.}. If we fix $\delta > 0$ small enough so that the first term can be combined with the left side of the above inequality (and absorbing $\delta$ into the constant $C$), one has that 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			\sup_{t \in [0,T]}\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t) \leq C\norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}(0) + C\left(\int_{0}^{T}\norm{F}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}(t)\;dt\right)^{2} + C\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\partial a}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\norm{\partial \phi}^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(t)\;dt.
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	Thus using Gr\"onwall's lemma concludes the proof. 
\end{proof}

\subsection{Solutions to the Linear Wave Equation (Constant Coefficients)} 
In the previous subsection, we outlined various simple energy estimates solutions to the (non-)constant coefficient linear wave equations would satisfy if they existed, and also used these estimates to establish uniqueness results. But we have not yet shown that such solutions exist, nor what their physical properties. We dedicate this subsection to answering these questions. 


Consider again the Cauchy wave problem (\ref{eq:clwp}), 
\begin{equation*}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{1}
		&\Box \phi = 0, \\
		&(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}), 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation*}
where we initially assume $\phi_{0}, \phi_{1} \in C_{c}^{\infty} \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, and $\Box$ denotes the \textbf{wave operator}. Then one has the following result. 

\begin{theorem}[Existence and Uniqueness for (\ref{eq:clwp})]
	Given initial data $(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}) \in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \subset \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, there exists a unique solution $\phi \in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}; \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))$ that solves (\ref{eq:clwp}).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
 Assuming that $\phi$ is an appropriate space of functions, taking the Fourier transformation of the wave equation yields, 
\begin{equation*}
	\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{\phi} + 4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\hat{\phi} = 0 \implies \partial_{t}^{2}\hat{\phi} = -4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\hat{\phi}, 
\end{equation*} 
which is a second-order ordinary differential equation. Thus solving this system, one obtains the general solution 
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{\phi}(t, \xi) = A\cos(2\pi t|\xi|) + B\sin(2\pi t \xi). 
\end{equation*}
Thus matching the initial conditions, it follows that 
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{\phi}(t, \xi) = \hat{\phi}_{0}\cos(2\pi t \xi) + \frac{\hat{\phi}_{1}}{2\pi  \xi}\sin(2\pi t \xi). 
\end{equation*}
But to complete the proof of existence, it remains to be seen that we can compute the inverse Fourier transform of $\hat{\phi}$. It suffices to show, at least, that $\hat{\phi}(t, \empspace) \in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$. Thus, we compute, 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)|\;d\xi &\leq \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\phi}_{0}(\xi)|\;d\xi + \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}|\sin(2\pi t |\xi|)|\;d\xi. 
	\end{split}
\end{align*}
Since $\hat{\phi}_{0}(\xi)$ is Schwartz, it is integrable, and thus the first term is finite. $\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)$ is also Schwartz, and thus bounded above; i.e., there exists some $M > 0$ such that for all $\xi$, $|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)| \leq M$. We note that $\sin(2\pi t \xi)/\xi \to t$ as $\xi \to 0$. This means there exists some $\epsilon > 0$ such that for all $\xi \in B_{\epsilon}(0)$, $|\sin(2\pi t \xi)/\xi - t| \lesssim_{t} 1$. This means that 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)|\;d\xi &\leq \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\phi}_{0}(\xi)|\;d\xi + \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}|\sin(2\pi t |\xi|)|\;d\xi \\
		&\lesssim \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{\phi}_{0}|\;d\xi + \int_{B_{\epsilon}(0)}M\;d\xi + \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(0)}|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|\;d\xi < \infty.  
	\end{split} 
\end{align*}
Therefore, it follows that $\hat{\phi}(t, \empspace) \in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$. The Fourier Inversion Theorem then tells us that 
\begin{equation*}
	\phi(t, x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi 
\end{equation*}
The final piece of the proof of existence is that $\phi(t, x)$ is a solution to (\ref{eq:clwp}). We first compute, 
\begin{equation*}
	\phi(0,x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\phi}(0, \xi)e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\phi}_{0}e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi = \phi_{0}(x). 	
\end{equation*}
Then using integrability and the dominated convergence theorem, one obtains: 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		\partial_{t}\phi(t, x) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi, \\
		\partial_{t}^{2}\phi(t, x) &= \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi, \\
		\Delta \phi(t, x) &= -\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi,  
	\end{split}
\end{align*}
so that 
\begin{equation*}
	\partial_{t}\phi(0,x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\;d\xi = \phi_{1}(x), 
\end{equation*}
and 
\begin{equation*}
	\Box \phi = \partial_{t}^{2}\phi(t,x) - \Delta \phi(t, x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{\phi}(t, \xi) + 4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\hat{\phi}(t, \xi)\;d\xi = 0. 
\end{equation*}
Thus, an integrable solution exists for (\ref{eq:clwp}) under the hypothesis assumed above. In fact, since $\hat{\phi}(t, x) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, one actually has that $\phi(t, \cdot) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$. To prove uniqueness, assume $\phi^{(1)}$ and $\phi^{(2)}$ are two solutions to (\ref{eq:clwp}). Linearity of $\Box$ then implies that $\phi \coloneqq \phi^{(1)} - \phi^{(2)}$ also solves the linear wave equation with zero initial data. Thus, if $E(t) = \norm{\partial \phi}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}$ denotes the energy of $\phi$, then $E(0) = 0$. Thus conservation of energy implies that $E(t) = 0$ for all $t \geq 0$, whence $\phi \equiv 0$. Therefore, $\phi^{(1)} \equiv \phi^{(2)}$. 
\end{proof}

In particular, one does not completely require the initial data to smooth with compact support. We have the following existence and uniqueness result. 

\begin{theorem}[Existence and Uniqueness for (\ref{eq:clwp})]
	Given initial data $(\phi, \partial_{t}\phi)\upharpoonright_{\{t = 0\}} = (\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}) \in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) \times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, there exists a unique solution $\phi \in L^{\infty}([0,T]; H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})) \times L^{\infty}([0,T]; L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))$ that solves (\ref{eq:clwp}). 
\end{theorem}

For $n \geq 2$, we intuitively expect the amplitude of the wave $|\phi(t, x)|$ to decay as $t \to \infty$ for the following reason: as $t \to \infty$, the support of the function increases (e.g., consider ripples on the surface of a lake), while the total energy of the wave is conserved. Note that this reasoning  does not quite apply to $n = 1$ since the size of the support remains constant. Now we can rigorously compute the decay estimates. We have the following first result. 

\begin{prop}[Pointwise Decay Rate]
	Fix any $R > 0$. For $(\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}) \in C_{c}^{\infty} \times C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ and $\phi$ the unique solution to the linear Cauchy wave problem, 
	\begin{equation}
		\sup_{|x| \leq R}|\phi(t, x)|\lesssim_{(\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}, R)} \frac{1}{(1 + t)^{n - 1}}. 
		\label{eq:decay_clwp_1}
	\end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
	The fundamental ideas behind the proof are as follows: we wish to consider the expression 
	\begin{equation*}
		\phi(t, x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{2\pi i (t|\xi| + x \cdot \xi)}\left(\frac{\hat{\phi}_{0}}{2} + \frac{\hat{\phi}_{1}}{2\pi i |\xi|}\right) + e^{-2\pi i t|\xi|}\left(\frac{\hat{\phi}_{0}}{2} - \frac{\hat{\phi}_{1}}{2\pi i |\xi|}\right)\;d\xi, 
	\end{equation*}
	and then bound each of the terms in the integral. For sake of demonstrating the main ideas, we shall assume $n = 3$. The proof follows trivially for all $|t| \leq 1$: let 
	\begin{equation*}
		\begin{matrix}
			\displaystyle I_{1} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{2\pi i (t|\xi| + x \cdot \xi)}\frac{\hat{\phi}_{0}}{2}\;d\xi, & & \displaystyle I_{2} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-2\pi i (t|\xi| + x \cdot \xi)}\frac{\hat{\phi}_{0}}{2}\;d\xi. \\ && \\
			\displaystyle I_{3}  = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{2\pi i (t|\xi| + x \cdot \xi)}\frac{\hat{\phi}_{1}}{2\pi i |\xi|}\;d\xi && \displaystyle I_{4} = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-2\pi i(t|\xi| + x \cdot \xi)}\frac{\hat{\phi}_{1}}{2\pi i |\xi|}\;d\xi. 
		\end{matrix}
	\end{equation*}
	Since $\hat{\phi}_{0}$ is Schwartz, and thus integrable, there exist $M_{1}, M_{2} > 0$ such that for all $|t|\leq 1$, $|I_{i}| \leq M_{i}$ for $i = 1, 2$. Now for $I_{3}$ (and analogously for $I_{4}$), we can write 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			|I_{3}| &\leq \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi = \int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi + \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi \\
			&\leq \int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{K_{1}}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi + \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi\\
			&= \int_{B_{1}(0)}\frac{K_{1}}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi + \int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B_{1}(0)}\frac{|\hat{\phi}_{1}(\xi)|}{2\pi |\xi|}\;d\xi < \infty, 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	where the second line follows from the fact that since $\hat{\phi}_{1} \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$, it is continuous on the compact set $\overline{B}_{1}(0)$ and thus bounded above by some positive constant $K_{1}$, and the final line follows from the fact that $1/|\xi|$ is integrable over $B_{1}(0)$ for all $n \geq 2$, and the second term in the final line is controlled above by H\"older's inequality; $I_{4}$ is controlled in the same way. Thus, one has that for every $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, and all $|t| \leq 1$, $|\phi(t, x)| \leq M$ for some constant $M$. Taking $C > 0$ large enough so that $C \geq M \cdot 2^{n - 1}$, one has that
	\begin{equation*}
		\sup_{|x| \leq R}|\phi(t, x)| \leq \frac{M}{(1 + t)^{2}}; 
	\end{equation*} 
	note that since $M_{1}, M_{2}, K_{1}, K_{2}$ are dependent on $\phi_{0}, \phi_{1}$, the proof concludes. So now we focus on the case with $t > 1$. Now introduce polar coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ as follows: let $(|\xi|, \xi_{\theta}, \xi_{\phi})$ be the polar coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\xi}$ with $\xi_{1} = |\xi|\sin{\xi_{\theta}}\cos{\xi_{\phi}}$, $\xi_{2} = |\xi|\sin{\xi_{\theta}}\sin{\xi_{\phi}}$, and $\xi_{3} = |\xi|\cos{\xi_{\theta}}$. The corresponding volume form is, 
	\begin{equation*}
		d\xi = |\xi|^{2}\sin{\xi_{\theta}}\;d|\xi|d\xi_{\theta}d\xi_{\phi}. 
	\end{equation*} 
	Furthermore, one has that for any $N \in \mathbb{N}_{0}$, 
	\begin{equation*}
		\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i t}\frac{\partial}{\partial |\xi|}\right)^{N}e^{2\pi i t|\xi|} = e^{2\pi i t|\xi|}. 
	\end{equation*}
	Now using integration by parts, 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			I &= \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^\infty e^{2\pi i t|\xi|} e^{2\pi i (x \cdot \xi)} \frac{\hat{\phi}_1(\xi)}{2\pi i}|\xi|\sin(\xi_\theta)\;d|\xi|d\xi_\theta d\xi_\varphi \\
			&= \left[ \frac{1}{(2\pi i)^2 t}\int_0^{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{2\pi i t |\xi|}e^{2\pi i (x \cdot \xi)}\hat{\phi}_1(\xi) |\xi| \sin(\xi_\theta)\;d\xi_\varphi d\xi_\theta \right]^{R \to \infty}_{|\xi| = 0} \\
			&\qquad - \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{2\pi i t} e^{2\pi i t|\xi|} \frac{\partial}{\partial |\xi|}\left(e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi}\frac{\hat{\phi}_1(\xi)}{2\pi i}|\xi|\right)\sin(\xi_\theta)\;d|\xi| d\xi_\theta d\xi_\varphi
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	We claim that the first term is identically zero. To prove this, we first note that 
	\begin{equation*}
		\lim_{|\xi|\to \infty}\abs{^{2\pi i t |\xi|}e^{2\pi i (x \cdot \xi)}\hat{\phi}_1(\xi) |\xi| \sin(\xi_\theta)} = \lim_{|\xi| \to \infty}|\hat{\phi}_1(\xi)||\xi| = 0, 
	\end{equation*}
	since $\hat{\phi}_1(\xi) \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^3)$. On the other hand, the integrand vanishes identically at $|\xi| = 0$. Now, we see that one application of integration by parts gave us a power of $t$. To get another power of $t$, we will use integration by parts on the second term. This time, after dealing with a boundary term at $|\xi| = 0$, we get 
	\begin{equation*}
		I = -\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}e^{2\pi i t|\xi|}\frac{1}{8\pi^3it^2}\left(\dotsm\right)\sin(\xi_\theta)\;d|\xi|d\xi_\theta d\theta_\varphi + \frac{1}{2\pi^2 i t^2}\hat{\phi}_1(\xi = 0). 
	\end{equation*}
	The boundary term does not allow us to use integration by parts again. Thus bounding each of the term and using the fact that $\hat{\phi}_1 \in \mathcal{S}$, one obtains 
	\begin{equation*}
		|I| \leq \frac{C(1 + R^2)}{t^2}. 
	\end{equation*}
\end{proof}

Note that this decay bound is not uniform for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.  We can actually get a uniform decay bound for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, but at the cost of the strength. 

\begin{theorem}[Uniform Decay Rate]
	For $(\phi_0, \phi_1) \in C_c^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n) \times C_c^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, and $\phi$ the unique solution to the homogeneous initial value problem, there exists $C = C(\phi_0, \phi_1) > 0$ so that
	\begin{equation*}
		\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}^n}|\phi(t,x)| \leq \frac{C}{(1 + t)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}
	\end{equation*}
	for $t\geq 0$. 
\end{theorem}

The key result is the following: for all $n \geq 2$, solutions to (\ref{eq:clwp}) undergo \textit{geometric dispersion}, while no dispersion occurs for $n = 1$.

\section{Explicit Representation Formulas} 
In this section, we work through the derivations of explicit representation formulas for initial value problems of the \textit{wave equation},
\begin{equation}
	\Box u\coloneqq u_{tt} - \Delta u = 0 \quad (\text{or more generally, } f), 
\end{equation}
where the unknown is the function $u: \bar{U} \times [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$, $u = u(x, t)$, with $x \in U^{\text{open}} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $t > 0$, $\Delta$ is taken with respect to the spatial variables $x = (x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n})$, and $f: U \times [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is a given function \cite{evans10}. 

\subsubsection{The Wave Equation in One Spatial Dimension -- D'Alembert's Formula}
The simplest example of the wave equation happens in the case $n = 1$. Consider the following initial value problem, 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - u_{xx} &= 0 &\qquad \text{ in $\mathbb{R} \times (0, \infty)$}  \\
		&u = g,  u_{t} &= h &\qquad \text{ on $\mathbb{R} \times \{t = 0\}$}. 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}
We note that the wave equation can be written in a more suggestive manner: 
\begin{equation}
	0 = u_{tt} - u_{xx} = (\partial_{t} + \partial_{x})(\partial_{t} - \partial_{x})u \eqqcolon (\partial_{t} + \partial_{x})v, 
\end{equation}
where we set $v = v(x, t) = u_{t} - u_{x}$. Thus, $v$ satisfies the transport equation, $v_{t} + v_{x} = 0$. Using the solution to the transport equation in one dimension, one obtains the solution, 
\begin{equation*}
	v(x, t) = a(x - t), 
\end{equation*}
where $a(x) \coloneqq v(x, 0)$. This gives us the nonhomogeneous transport equation, 
\begin{equation*}
	u_{t} -u_{x} = a(x -t), 
\end{equation*}
for which the explicit representation formula was shown (see Appendix) to be 
\begin{equation}
	u(x, t) = \int_{0}^{t}a(x + (t - s)- s)\;ds + g(x + t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{x - t}^{x + t}a(y)\;dy + g(x + t). 
\end{equation}
It remains for us to identify the function $a(x, t)$. Using the definition of $v(x, t)$, at $t = 0$, one has, 
\begin{equation*}
	a(x) = v(x,0) = u_{t}(x, 0) - u_{x}(x, 0) = h(x) - g'(x). 
\end{equation*}
Therefore, using the fundamental theorem of calculus and the above results, we conclude that 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{u(x, t) = \frac{1}{2}\left[g(x + t) - g(x - t)\right] + \frac{1}{2}\int_{x - t}^{x + t}h(y)\;dy} \qquad (x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}, t \geq 0). 
\end{equation}
This explicit formula is called \textbf{d'Alembert's formula} for $n = 1$. Hence, we have shown that \textit{if} the wave equation has a ``sufficiently smooth solution $u$'', then it must be given by d'Alembert's formula. The following theorem formalizes this result. 

\begin{theorem}[Solution to the Wave Equation ($n = 1$)]
	Assume $g \in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})$, $h \in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})$, and define $u$ by d'Alembert's formula. Then \vspace{-0.25cm}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\roman{*})]
		\item $u \in C^{2}(\mathbb{R} \times [0, \infty))$. 
		\item $u_{tt} - u_{xx} = 0$ in $\mathbb{R} \times (0, \infty)$ 
		\item $\lim\limits_{\substack{(x, t) \to (x^{0}, 0) \\ t > 0}}u(x, t) = g(x^{0})$ and $\lim\limits_{\substack{(x, t) \to (x^{0}, 0) \\ t > 0}}u_{t}(x, t) = h(x^{0})$ for each point $x^{0} \in \mathbb{R}$. 
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\newpage 
\begin{proof}
	$ $\newline \vspace{-0.65cm}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\roman{*})]
		\item Define $u$ by d'Alembert's formula. Then we compute the following: 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				u_{x} &= \frac{1}{2}[g'(x + t) + g'(x - t)] + \frac{1}{2}[h(x + t) - h(x - t)]. \\
				u_{t} &= \frac{1}{2}[g'(x + t) -g'(x - t)] + \frac{1}{2}[h(x + t) + h(x - t)], 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		whence 
		\begin{align*}
			\begin{split}
				u_{xx} &= \frac{1}{2}[g''(x + t) + g''(x - t)] + \frac{1}{2}[h'(x + t) - h'(x - t)]. \\ 
				u_{xt} &= \frac{1}{2}[g''(x + t) - g''(x - t)] + \frac{1}{2}[h'(x + t) + h'(x - t)].  \\
				u_{tt} &= \frac{1}{2}[g''(x + t) +  g''(x - t)] + \frac{1}{2}[h'(x + t) - h'(x - t)]. 
			\end{split}
		\end{align*}
		Since $g', g''$ and $h, h'$ are all continuous at every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and $t \in [0, \infty)$, as are the functions $x + t$ and $x - t$, we conclude that each of the first and second-order partial derivatives of $u$ are continuous on $\mathbb{R} \times [0, \infty)$. Thus, $u \in C^{2}(\mathbb{R} \times [0,\infty))$. 
		\item From the above calculations, it follows immediately that $u_{tt} - u_{xx} = 0$. 
		\item By (1), $u$ is continuous at every $(x, t) \in \mathbb{R} \times [0, \infty)$, and thus continuous at every point of the form $(x^{0}, 0) \in \mathbb{R} \times \{0\}$. Thus by the limit definition of continuity, one has 
		\begin{equation*}
			\lim_{(x, t) \to (x^{0}, 0)}u(x, t) = u(x^{0}, 0) \eqqcolon g(x^{0}). 
		\end{equation*}
		Likewise, by our remark above, $u_{t}(x, t) \in C^{1}(\mathbb{R} \times [0, \infty)$. Thus by the limit definition of continuity, one has 
		\begin{equation*}
			\lim_{(x, t) \to (x^{0}, 0)} u_{t}(x, t) = u_{t}(x^{0}, 0) \eqqcolon h(x^{0}). 
		\end{equation*}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{The Wave Equation in One Spatial Dimension -- Half-Line}
Now we will consider the modified initial value problem, 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - u_{xx} &=0 &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times (0, \infty)$} \\
		&u = g, u_{t} &=h &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times \{t = 0\}$}) \\
		&u = 0 & &\qquad (\text{on $\{x = 0\} \times (0,\infty)$}. 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}
To solve this problem, we will use the \textit{method of reflections}; i.e., we will extend the initial value problem to the entire real line by means of the odd reflections: 
\begin{equation*}
	\ti{u}(x, t) = 
	\begin{cases}
		u(x, t) & x \geq 0 \\
		-u(-x,t) & x \leq 0.  
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
	\ti{g}(x) = 
	\begin{cases}
		g(x), & x \geq 0 \\
		-g(-x), & x \leq 0,
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
	\ti{h}(x) = 
	\begin{cases}
		h(x), & x \geq 0 \\
		-h(-x), & x \leq 0. 
	\end{cases}
\end{equation*}
It is straightforward to check that $\ti{u}, \ti{g}, \ti{h}$ satisfy the ordinary wave equation ($n= 1$) on the whole real line. Thus by d'Alembert's formula, we obtain the following solution: 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{u(x, t) = 
		\left\{
		\begin{alignedat}{2}
			&\frac{1}{2}[g(x + t) - g(x-t)] + \frac{1}{2}\int_{x -t}^{x + t}h(y)\;dy &\qquad (x\geq t \geq 0)\\
			&\frac{1}{2}[g(x + t) - g(t - x)] + \frac{1}{2}\int_{t - x}^{x + t}h(y)\;dy &\qquad (0 \leq x \leq t)
		\end{alignedat}
		\right.
	}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{The Wave Equation in \texorpdfstring{$n\geq 2$}{>2} Spatial Dimension -- Kirchoff's and Poisson's Formulas}
In this subsection, we will develop explicit representation formulas for the wave equation for dimensions $n \geq 2$. To develop these formulas, we require the \textit{method of spherical means}. Consider the following definitions. 

\begin{definition}[Spherical Means]
	$ $\newline \vspace{-0.25cm}
	\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt,label = (\roman{*})]
		\item Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, $t > 0$, and $r > 0$. We define the average of $u(\cdot, t)$ over the sphere $\partial B(x, r)$ to be 
		\begin{equation}
			U(x;r, t) = \dashint_{\partial B(x, r)} u(y,t)\;dS(y).
		\end{equation}
		\item We make the analogous definitions, 
		\begin{equation}
			\begin{cases}
				G(x; r) &= \dashint_{\partial B(x,r)}g(y)\;dS(y). \\
				H(x; r) &= \dashint_{\partial B(x,r)}h(y)\;dS(y). 
			\end{cases}
		\end{equation}
	\end{enumerate}
	For any fixed $x$, we will regard $U, G, H$ as functions of $r$ and $t$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Euler-Poisson-Darboux Equation]
	Fix $x \in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, $n, m \geq 2$, and let $u \in C^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n} \times [0, \infty))$ satisfy the initial value problem, 
	\begin{equation*}
		\left\{
		\begin{alignedat}{3}
			&u_{tt} - \Delta u &= 0 &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n} \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
			&u = g, u_{t} &= h &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n} \times \{t = 0\}$}). 
		\end{alignedat}
		\right. 
	\end{equation*}
	Then $U \in C^{m}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+} \times [0,\infty))$, and $U_{tt}$ satisfies the \textit{Euler-Poisson-Darboux} initial value problem, 
	\begin{equation}
		\left\{
		\begin{alignedat}{3}
			&U_{tt} - U_{rr} - \frac{n-1}{r}U_{r} &= 0 & \qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times (0,\infty)$)} \\
			&U = G, U_{t} = H& &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times \{t = 0\}$}
		\end{alignedat}
		\right. 
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	We begin by taking the partial derivative of the spherical mean $U$ with respect to $r$. To do so, we rewrite 
	\begin{equation*}
		U(x;r,t) = \dashint_{\partial B(x, r)}u(y,t)\;dS(y) = \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)r^{n-1}}\int_{\partial B(x, r)}u(y,t)\;dS(y) = \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}u(x + rz, t)\;dS(z). 
	\end{equation*}
	Thus, 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			U_{r}(x;r, t) &= \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}u(x + rz, t)\;dS(z) \\
			&= \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}Du(x + rz, t) \cdot z \;dS(z) \\
			&= \frac{r^{1-n}}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{\partial B(x, r)}Du(y, t) \cdot \frac{y -x}{r}\;dS(y) \\
			&= \frac{r}{n\alpha(n)r^{n}}\int_{\partial B(x, r)}\frac{\partial u}{\partial \nu}\;dS(y) \\
			&= \frac{r}{n\alpha(n)r^{n}}\int_{B(x, r)}\Delta u\;dy = \frac{r}{n}\dashint_{B(x, r)}\Delta u\;dy,
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	where passing the derivative inside the integral in the second line is justified since $u$ is sufficiently smooth (meaning that $Du$ exists a.e. and is integrable on $\partial B(0,1)$), and the final line follows from Green's formula. Now taking some large $R > 0$, and restricting to $0 < r \leq R/2$, one has that since $B(x,r) \subset \overline{B(x, R/2)}$ and $\Delta u$ is continuous (so that the extreme value theorem applies), 
	\begin{equation*}
		\norm{\Delta u}_{L^{\infty}(B(x, r))} \leq \norm{\Delta u}_{L^{\infty}(B(x, R))} \eqqcolon C < \infty. 
	\end{equation*}
	Hence, 
	\begin{equation*}
		U_{r}(x;r, t) \leq \frac{C}{n}r \implies \lim_{r \to 0^{+}}U_{r}(x;r, t) = 0.
	\end{equation*}
	Now we will compute the second partial derivative of $U$ with respect to $r$. We compute, 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			U_{rr}(x;r, t) &= \frac{1 -n}{n\alpha(n)r^{n}}\int_{B(x, r)}\Delta u\;dy + \frac{r^{1-n}}{n\alpha(n)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int_{B(x, r)}\Delta u(y,t)\;dy \\
			&= \frac{1 - n}{n}\dashint_{B(x, r)}\Delta u(y,t)\;dy + \frac{r^{1-n}}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{\partial B(x, r)}\Delta u(y,t)\;dS(y) \\
			&= \dashint_{\partial B(x, r)}\Delta u(y, t)\;dS(y) + \left(\frac{1}{n} - 1\right)\dashint_{B(x, r)}\Delta u(y, t)\;dy, 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	where the second line follows from the coarea formula (see Appendix). By applying similar reasoning to above, one computes that $\lim_{r \to 0^{+}}U_{rr}(x;r, t) = (1/n)\Delta u$. In this manner, repeatedly differentiating $U$, one sees that $U \in C^{m}(\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+} \times [0, \infty))$. Now differentiating, 
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			(n-1)r^{n - 2}U_{r} + r^{n-1}U_{rr} &= \frac{d}{dr}(r^{n-1}U_{r}) \\
			&= \frac{d}{dr}\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{B(x,r)}u_{tt}\;dy  \\
			&= \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{\partial B(x,r)}u_{tt}\;dS(y)  \\
			&= r^{n-1}\dashint_{\partial B(x, r)}u_{tt}\;dS(y) \eqqcolon r^{n-1}U_{tt}, 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	with the third line following from the coarea formula. 
\end{proof}

Now we will derive the explicit representation formula for the 3-dimensional wave equation by reducing the system to the one-dimensional Euler-Poisson-Darboux equation and solving this system using the formula we derived for the one-dimensional half-line equation. 

\paragraph{Kirchoff's Formula for the Wave Equation \texorpdfstring{($n = 3$)}{n =3}}
Suppose $u \in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3} \times [0,\infty))$ solves the initial-value problem 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - \Delta u &= 0 &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{3} \times [0,\infty)$}) \\
		&u = g, u_{t} &=h &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{3} \times \{t = 0\}$})
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}
Define the functions, $\ti{U} = rU(x;r, t)$, $\ti{G}(r) = rG(x;r)$, and $\ti{H}(r) = rH(x;r)$. Then we observe the following: 
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep =-2pt, label = (\roman{*})]
	\item $ $\newline \vspace{-0.75cm}
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{split}
			\ti{U}_{tt} &= rU_{tt} = r\left[U_{rr} + \frac{2}{r}U_{r}\right] \\
			&= rU_{rr} + 2U_{r} = (U + rU_{r})_{r} = \ti{U}_{rr}. 
		\end{split}
	\end{align*}
	\item On $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times \{t = 0\}$, $U = G$ and $U_{t} = H$ so that $\ti{U} =\ti{G}$ and $\ti{U}_{t} = \ti{H}$. 
	\item For $r = 0$, $\partial B(x, 0) = \varnothing$ so that $\ti{U} = 0$ on $\{r = 0\} \times (0, \infty)$. 
\end{enumerate}
In other words, we have shown that $\ti{U}$ satisfies the following initial value problem, 
\begin{equation*}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&\ti{U}_{tt} - \ti{U}_{rr} &= 0 &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
		&\ti{U} = \ti{G}, \ti{U}_{t} &= \ti{H} &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}_{+} \times \{t = 0\}$}) \\
		&\ti{U} = 0 &&\qquad (\text{on $\{r = 0\} \times (0,\infty)$})
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation*}
Using the solution for the one-dimensional wave equation on the half-line, for $0 \leq r \leq t$, one has 
\begin{equation*}
	\ti{U}(x; r, t) = \frac{1}{2}[\ti{G}(r + t) - \ti{G}(t - r)] + \frac{1}{2}\int_{t -r}^{r + t}\ti{H}(y)\;dy. 
\end{equation*}
On the other hand, one has that 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		u(x, t) &= \lim_{r \to 0^{+}}\frac{\ti{U}(x; r, t)}{r} \\
		&= \lim_{r \to 0^{+}}\left[\frac{\ti{G}(r + t) - \ti{G}(t - r)}{2r} + \frac{1}{2r}\int_{t - r}^{r + t}\ti{H}(y)\;dy\right] \\
		&= \ti{G}'(t) + \ti{H}(t). 
	\end{split}
\end{align*}
Recall again that 
\begin{align}
	\begin{split}
		\ti{G}'(t) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(t\dashint_{\partial B(x, t)}g(y)\;dS(y)\right) \\
		&= \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)t}\int_{\partial B(x ,t)}g(y)\;dS(y)\right) \\
		&= -\frac{1}{n\alpha(n)t^{2}}\int_{\partial B(x,t)}g(y)\;dS(y) + \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(t^{2}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}g(x + tz)\;dS(z)\right) \\
		&= -\dashint_{\partial B(x, t)}g(y)\;dS(y) + \frac{2}{n\alpha(n)}\int_{\partial B(0,1)}g(x + tz)\;dS(z) + \frac{1}{n\alpha(n)t}\int_{\partial B(x, t)}Dg(y) \cdot \left(\frac{y - x}{t}\right)\;dS(y) \\
		&= \dashint_{\partial B(x, t)}g(y)\;dS(y) + \dashint_{\partial B(x, t)}Dg(y) \cdot (y - x)\;dS(y). 
	\end{split}
\end{align}
Likewise, one has 
\begin{equation*}
	\ti{H}(t) = tH(t) = \dashint_{\partial B(x, t)}th(y)\;dS(y). 
\end{equation*}
Therefore, we conclude that 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{u(x, t) = \dashint_{\partial B(x, t)}th(y) + g(y) + Dg(y) \cdot (y - x)\;dS(y)}\label{eq:Kirchoff}
\end{equation}
This is \textbf{Kirchoff's formula} for the wave equation in dimension 3.

\paragraph{Poisson's Formula for the Wave Equation \texorpdfstring{($n = 2$)}{n=2}} Assume $u \in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2} \times [0,\infty))$ solves the IVP for $n = 2$. Unlike for the $n = 3$ case, we will consider the 2-dimensional problem and ``view'' it as a 3-dimensional problem. Define $\bar{u}(x_{1}, x_{2}, x_{3}, t) = u(x_{1}, x_{2}, t)$ so that $u$ satisfies the IVP 
\begin{equation*}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&\bar{u}_{tt} - \Delta \bar{u} &= 0 &\qquad(\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{3} \times (0, \infty)$}) \\
		&\bar{u} =\bar{g}, \bar{u}_{t} &= \bar{h} &\qquad(\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{3} \times \{t = 0\}$}), 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation*}
where we define $\bar{g}$ and $\bar{h}$ analogously. For $x = (x_{1}, x_{2}) \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$, write $\bar{x} = (x_{1}, x_{2}, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$. Then by Kirchoff's formula, one has 
\begin{equation*}
	u(x, t) = \bar{u}(\bar{x}, t) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(t\dashint_{\partial \bar{B}(\bar{x},t)}\bar{g}d\bar{S}\right) + t\dashint_{\partial \bar{B}(\bar{x},t)}\bar{h}d\bar{S}, 
\end{equation*}
where $\bar{B}(\bar{x}, t)$ denotes the ball in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ with center $\bar{x}$, radius $t > 0$, and where $d\bar{S}$ denotes the 2-dimensional surface measure on $\partial \bar{B}(\bar{x}, t)$. But we can write 
\begin{equation*}
	\dashint_{\partial \bar{B}(\bar{x},t)}\bar{g}d\bar{S} = \frac{1}{4\pi t^{2}}\int_{\partial \bar{B}(\bar{x}, t)}\bar{g}d\bar{S} = \frac{2}{4\pi t^{2}}\int_{B(x,t)}g(y)(1 + |D\gamma(y)|^{2})^{1/2}\;dy, 
\end{equation*}
where $\gamma(y) = (t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}$ for $y \in B(x, t)$. The factor 2 comes from the two hemispheres of $\partial\bar{B}(\bar{x}, t)$. Since $(1 + |D\gamma|^{2})^{1/2} = t(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{-1/2}$, one has 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		\dashint_{\partial \bar{B}(\bar{x}, t)}\bar{g}d\bar{S} &= \frac{1}{2\pi t}\int_{B(x,t)}\frac{g(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy \\
		&= \frac{t}{2}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{g(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy.
	\end{split}
\end{align*}
Using this and the coarea formula, one obtains 
\begin{equation*}
	u(x, t) = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(t^{2}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{g(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy\right) + \frac{t^{2}}{2}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{h(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy.
\end{equation*}
Using the same reasoning as above to pass the derivative inside the integral, one has 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(t^{2}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{g(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy\right) &= t\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{g(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy + t\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{Dg(y) \cdot (y -x)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy. 
	\end{split}
\end{align*}
Thus for $n = 2$, we obtain \textbf{Poisson's Formula}, 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{ tg(y) + t^{2}h(y) + tDg(y) \cdot (y -x) }{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy \qquad \quad (x \in \mathbb{R}^{2}, t > 0)
	}
\end{equation}
Notice that in solving the wave equation for $n = 2$, we used the \textit{method of descent}; i.e., we solved the problem for $n = 3$ first, and then dropped down to $n =2$. 

\paragraph{General Solutions for \texorpdfstring{$n\geq 2$}{n > 1}} Following similar extensive calculations as above (see Appendix for the work), we obtain the following solutions to the wave equation, 
\begin{equation}
	\boxed{
		u(x, t) = 
		\left\{
		\begin{alignedat}{2}
			&\frac{1}{\gamma(n)}\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)\left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\left(t^{n-2}\dashint_{\partial B(x,t)}g\;dS\right) + \left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\left(t^{n-2}\dashint_{\partial B(x,t)}h\;dS\right)\right] &\qquad \substack{\displaystyle n \geq 3 \\ \displaystyle n \text{ odd}} \\
			&\frac{1}{\beta(n)}\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)\left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(t^{n}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{g(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy\right)\right. &\\
			&\qquad \qquad \qquad \left.+ \left(\frac{1}{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(t^{n}\dashint_{B(x,t)}\frac{h(y)}{(t^{2} - |y - x|^{2})^{1/2}}\;dy\right)\right] &\qquad \substack{\displaystyle n \geq 2 \\ \displaystyle n \text{ even}}
		\end{alignedat}
		\right.
	}
\end{equation}
where in the first equation $\gamma_{n} = 1 \cdot 3 \cdot \dotsm\cdot (n-2)$, and $\beta(n) = 2 \cdot 4 \cdot \dotsm \cdot (n-2) \cdot n$. The above solutions highlight an interesting contrast between odd-dimensional, and even-dimensional solutions. Note that if $n$ is odd and $n \geq 3$, the initial data at a point $x\in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ only affect the solution $u$ along the boundary $\{(y,t) \mid t > 0, |x -y| = t\}$ of the cone $C = \{(y,t)\mid t > 0, |x - y| < t\}$. On the other hand, if $n$ is given, the initial data can affect the solution within all of $C$. Thus, a disturbance originating at $x$ propagates along a sharp wavefront in odd dimensions, but continues to have effects even after the leading edge of the wavefront passes in even dimensions. This phenomenon is \textbf{Huygen's principle}. 

\subsection{Solution to the Nonhomogeneous Wave Equation}
Now we will use the solutions from the previous sections to address the nonhomogeneous wave equation, 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - \Delta u &= f &\qquad (\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n} \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
		&u =0, u_{t} &= 0 &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{n} \times \{t = 0\}$}). 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}
To solve this problem, we use Duhamel's principle\footnote{See \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duhamel\%27s\_principle}{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duhamel\%27s\_principle} for an explanation.} by setting $u = u(x,t;s)$ to be the solution of 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt}(\cdot; s)- \Delta u(\cdot, s) &= 0 \qquad &(\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n} \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
		&u(\cdot;s) =0, u_{t}(\cdot;s) &= f(\cdot,s) \qquad &(\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{n} \times \{t = 0\}$}). 
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}
Thus, by Duhamel's principle, 
\begin{equation}
	u(x, t) \coloneqq \int_{0}^{t}u(x,t;s)\;ds \qquad (x \in \mathbb{R}^n, t \geq 0)
\end{equation}
is a solution of the above nonhomogeneous wave equation. Consequently, the solution to the general inhomogeneous wave problem, 
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - \Delta u &= f &\qquad(\text{in $\mathbb{R}^n \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
		&u = g, u_t &=h &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}^n \times \{t = 0\}$})
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}
is equal to the sum of the solutions to the problems
\begin{equation}
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - \Delta u &= 0 &\qquad(\text{in $\mathbb{R}^n \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
		&u = g, u_t &=h &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}^n \times \{t = 0\}$})
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
	\quad 
	\text{and} 
	\quad 
	\left\{
	\begin{alignedat}{3}
		&u_{tt} - \Delta u &= f &\qquad(\text{in $\mathbb{R}^n \times (0,\infty)$}) \\
		&u = 0, u_t &=0 &\qquad (\text{on $\mathbb{R}^n \times \{t = 0\}$})
	\end{alignedat}
	\right.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Example 1: Solution to Nonhomogeneous Problem for \texorpdfstring{$n = 1$}{n=1}}
By d'Alembert's formula, one has 
\begin{equation*}
	u(x,t;s) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{x -t + s}^{x + t + s}f(y,s)\;dy. 
\end{equation*}
Thus, 
\begin{equation*}
	u(x,t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{x - t + s}^{x + t + s}f(y,s)\;dyds = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^t\int_{x - s}^{x + s}f(y,t-s)\;dyds \qquad (x \in \mathbb{R}, t \geq 0).
\end{equation*}

\subsubsection{Example 2: Solution to Nonhomogeneous Problem for \texorpdfstring{$n = 3$}{n=3}}

By Kirchoff's formula, one has 
\begin{equation*}
	u(x,t;s) = (t-s)\dashint_{\partial B(x,t -s)}f(y,s)\;dS. 
\end{equation*}
This implies that 
\begin{align*}
	\begin{split}
		u(x, t) &= \int_{0}^{t}(t - s)\dashint_{\partial B(x, t -s)}f(y,s)\;dSds \\
		&= \int_{0}^{t}(t - s)\frac{1}{4\pi(t - s)^{2}}\int_{\partial B(x, t - s)}f(y,s)\;dSds \\
		&= \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial B(x, t -s)}\frac{f(y,s)}{t - s}\;dSds \\
		&= \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\frac{f(y, t - r)}{r}\;dSdr. 
	\end{split}
\end{align*}
Hence, we conclude that 
\begin{equation*}
	u(x,t) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{B(x,t)}\frac{f(y, t - |y - x|)}{|y - x|}\;dy \qquad (x \in \mathbb{R}^3, t \geq 0), 
\end{equation*}
where the integrand on the right is called a \textit{retarded potential}.
